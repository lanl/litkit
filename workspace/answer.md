**Follicular dendritic cells (FDCs)**  

* **Virus trap and protection** – FDCs in lymphoid tissue bind HIV‐1 particles through multivalent ligand‐receptor (often complement‐mediated) interactions and keep the virions in their native, infectious form for months.  In vivo, virus trapped on as few as ≈ 100 FDCs remained infectious for at least 9 months, and in vitro human FDCs preserved infectivity for ≥ 25 days, whereas free virus lost infectivity within days[1][1].  

* **Source of virus during therapy** – When antiretroviral therapy (ART) is started, the plasma viral load falls rapidly (first phase) and then more slowly (second phase).  Mathematical models that include reversible multivalent binding of HIV to FDCs can reproduce this biphasic decay without invoking long‐lived infected cells; the slower second phase is largely the result of virus dissociating from FDCs[2].  Analyses of clinical data show that the amount of virus released from FDCs during treatment can exceed that produced by productively infected cells, forcing upward revisions of the estimated clearance rate of free virus (c) and the death rate of infected cells (δ)[3].  

* **Contribution to later‐phase viremia** – After the first few months of ART, low‐level plasma viremia persists for years.  Modeling of the third decay phase (half‐life ≈ 39 months) indicates that leakage of virus from FDCs can fully account for this long‐term “residual” viremia, and may continue to do so even beyond the fourth phase[4].  

* **Potential for rebound** – Because some virions bind many ligands, their dissociation from FDCs can take years, creating a reservoir that could reignite infection if therapy is stopped[5].  Rebinding of virions that have diffused away can further prolong retention on FDCs[6].  

**Latently infected cells**  

* **Traditional view** – The slow second (and later) phases of viral decay under ART have often been attributed to long‐lived, latently infected CD4+ T cells that sporadically produce virus.  

* **Model‐based reassessment** – The same multivalent‐binding models that explain the biphasic decay attribute the second phase primarily to release of FDC‐bound virus, not to latent cell activation.  Consequently, the contribution of latently infected cells to plasma viremia during therapy is likely smaller than previously thought[2][3].  

* **Implications for infected‐cell turnover** – Because the observed decay can be explained by FDC release, the death rate of productively infected cells (δ) and the clearance rate of free virus (c) are faster than earlier estimates that assumed a dominant latent‐cell source[3].  

**Summary**  

- **FDCs act as a durable physical reservoir** that protects HIV, slowly releases infectious virions during ART, and can dominate both the second‐phase decay and the very low‐level viremia that persists for years.  
- **Latently infected cells, while still present, are not required to explain the biphasic or later decay kinetics** observed under ART; their role in maintaining plasma virus during treatment appears modest compared with the continual leakage of virus from FDCs.
